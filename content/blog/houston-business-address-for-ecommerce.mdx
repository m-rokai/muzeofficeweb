## Start with the job the address must do

E-commerce sellers often use “virtual address,” “virtual mailbox,” and “virtual office” interchangeably. Providers do not always mean the same thing by those terms. Before comparing monthly prices, write down which of these jobs you need:

- **Customer returns:** Can the provider accept parcels returned by customers, including packages with prepaid labels or a different name on the label?
- **Supplier and sample deliveries:** Can it receive UPS, FedEx, DHL, and other private-carrier packages, or only USPS letter mail?
- **Marketplace and payment records:** Does the platform ask for a mailing address, a physical operating address, or documentation showing where inventory is held?
- **Business correspondence:** Do you need to receive bank statements, tax notices, checks, invoices, and signed documents?
- **Remote access:** Will you pick mail up in Houston, or do you need scanning and forwarding to another city or state?
- **Privacy:** Are you trying to keep your home address off customer-facing pages, invoices, public records, and business correspondence?

The answer may be a commercial mail-receiving service, a fulfillment partner, a registered agent, a warehouse, or a combination. An address service is not a substitute for inventory storage or order fulfillment unless the provider expressly offers those services in writing.

## Business address, registered office, and registered agent are different

The most important distinction is between an address used for business correspondence and the address used for legal service.

### Mailing or business address

A provider may give you a commercial street address where it receives mail on your behalf. Depending on the plan, it may notify you, scan the outside of an envelope, forward mail, or receive packages. You can use that address where a contract, marketplace, bank, or agency permits that type of address.

Ask the recipient what it accepts before changing an account. A street-format address does not guarantee approval, and a provider should not promise that every bank, marketplace, payment processor, or government form will treat it as a physical operating location.

### Texas registered office and registered agent

If your company is a Texas entity, the registered office is the Texas street address where the appointed registered agent can receive service of process during business hours. The registered agent is the person or organization accepting that legal service; the mailbox provider is not automatically the agent.

Texas’s [registered-agent guidance](https://www.sos.state.tx.us/corp/registeredagents.shtml) explains the role, and the [Texas Form 205 instructions](https://www.sos.state.tx.us/corp/instructions/205.shtml) describe the registered office and agent information used in a certificate of formation. A Houston mail service that is not appointed and operating as your registered agent does not take that role simply because it has a Houston address.

For a side-by-side comparison of a PO Box, virtual office, and registered-agent requirement, read our [Texas virtual office vs. PO Box guide](/blog/virtual-office-vs-po-box-in-texas).

### IRS mailing address and physical location

The IRS distinguishes between where it should mail correspondence and where a business is physically located. The [SS-4 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/iss4) explain the mailing-address and street-address fields. A commercial mail address may be appropriate for a mailing field when the IRS form permits it, but it does not turn a remote e-commerce operation into a Houston storefront or warehouse.

Complete federal and state forms according to the instructions for the specific field. If your inventory, staff, or operations are somewhere else, do not replace the physical-location answer with a mailbox address just because the format looks more professional.

## What to confirm about packages and customer returns

Letter-mail handling is usually the simplest service. E-commerce creates harder questions, so get written answers before directing customers or suppliers to the address.

### Carriers and signatures

Ask which carriers are accepted, whether a signature-required delivery can be signed for, and what happens when a driver arrives outside staffed hours. “Package receiving” may mean only certain carriers, locations, or plan tiers. Confirm whether USPS, UPS, FedEx, DHL, Amazon Logistics, and freight deliveries are included for your use case.

### Size, weight, and volume limits

Ask about maximum dimensions, weight, daily or monthly package counts, oversized parcels, pallets, hazardous materials, temperature-sensitive goods, and items requiring special handling. A provider that accepts ordinary parcels may reject a large return, a pallet, or a high-volume delivery. Also ask whether storage time starts when the package arrives and what daily storage charges apply.

### Customer returns and name variants

Your storefront name, legal entity, personal name, brand names, and supplier names may all appear on labels. Confirm which names can be authorized and whether each one must be listed in advance. Ask how the provider handles a return addressed to a customer name, a former brand, or a marketplace-generated label.

Do not tell customers to ship returns until you know the provider accepts them. A returned parcel that is refused, sent back, or held past its storage window becomes a customer-service problem as well as a shipping cost.

### Notifications, scanning, and forwarding

Find out whether notifications identify the sender, package type, tracking number, or only the arrival date. If you need remote access, ask:

- Does “scanning” mean the envelope exterior or the contents after authorization?
- How quickly are scans uploaded?
- How often are mail and packages forwarded?
- Is postage charged separately?
- Are per-item handling, scan, pick-and-pack, or forwarding fees charged?
- Can a package be consolidated with other mail?

Mail forwarding is not the same as fulfillment. It moves items to you; it does not necessarily inspect, relabel, store, or ship inventory to your customers.

## Address format: street address, suite, PMB, or #

A provider may advertise a street address with a suite number, but the address still needs to be represented accurately. USPS [Publication 28 guidance for private mailboxes](https://pe.usps.com/text/pub28/pub28c2_040.htm) describes the use of a private mailbox designator such as “PMB” or “#.” Follow the provider’s USPS-approved format rather than changing a PMB into “Suite” to make it appear to be an office.

Ask these questions before printing labels or updating a marketplace profile:

1. Is the location a Commercial Mail Receiving Agency (CMRA)?
2. What exact address and unit format should customers use?
3. Should the business name, owner name, or both appear on the label?
4. Does the provider permit customer returns and commercial-carrier deliveries?
5. Are there separate addresses for letter mail, packages, or pickup?

An accurate PMB is better than an inaccurate suite number. Mislabeling can delay mail, confuse carriers, and create a mismatch with the provider’s records.

## USPS Form 1583 is part of the setup

Before a CMRA receives USPS mail for you, you generally complete [USPS PS Form 1583](https://about.usps.com/forms/ps1583.pdf) and provide the identification the current form requires. The form identifies the addressee and authorizes delivery through the commercial mail agency. Your provider’s onboarding process should explain how it reviews the form and when service becomes active.

Do not assume that submitting the form means every package or private carrier is covered. Form 1583 addresses the USPS mail-receiving relationship; carrier acceptance, package storage, forwarding, and return handling are separate provider terms. Wait for the provider to confirm activation before changing labels, customer instructions, or government records.

## Privacy without overpromising

A business address can reduce how often you publish your home address, but it does not erase information already in public filings, historical records, domain registrations, shipping records, or data-broker databases. Review the privacy effect field by field:

- Which address appears on your Texas formation documents, and which address fields does the Comptroller request in its [Public Information Report guidance](https://comptroller.texas.gov/help/franchise/public-info-report.php)?
- Which address does your bank require as the business’s physical location?
- Which address does your marketplace display to customers for returns?
- Which address appears on invoices, packing slips, and your website?
- Who can access incoming mail, return labels, and package information?

Ask about access controls, employee handling, abandoned packages, returned mail, and document destruction. A provider should explain how it protects mail, but you remain responsible for deciding what information you publish and where.

## A virtual address does not automatically qualify for Google Business Profile

If your goal is a Google Business Profile, a mail-only address is not enough. Google’s [business representation guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177?hl=en) require the business to meet its eligibility rules. Depending on the business model, that can include genuinely operating at the location, receiving customers during stated hours, staffing the location with the business’s own team, and maintaining permanent signage.

An e-commerce seller that only receives mail or returns at a Houston address should not assume it can publish that address as a storefront. A coworking or office arrangement may support eligibility only when the business truly operates there and satisfies Google’s requirements. ## Houston e-commerce address decision checklist

Before you sign up or redirect a customer, confirm each item in writing:

- The exact address format, including PMB, suite, or unit notation
- Which business and brand names may receive mail
- USPS, UPS, FedEx, DHL, Amazon, and freight-carrier policies
- Package size, weight, volume, signature, and prohibited-item limits
- Customer-return acceptance and refused-package procedures
- Notification timing, scanning scope, forwarding schedule, postage, and handling fees
- Pickup hours, after-hours access, holiday closures, and storage deadlines
- What happens if the account closes or a package is unclaimed
- Whether the address is suitable for the specific bank, marketplace, form, or license you are completing
- Whether you still need a separate Texas registered agent, warehouse, fulfillment partner, or physical operating site
